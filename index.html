Black Hole Lab · Schwarzschild, non-rotating

Every pixel here is one ray of light, bent by gravity.

A black hole and its disk of hot gas, drawn live. Nothing in the image is painted in. For each pixel, your graphics card follows a ray of light backward through curved spacetime with the orbit equation from general relativity, then asks what that ray came from.

One equation draws the whole picture

u″ + ua straight line = ³⁄₂ u²gravity

Here u is rₛ divided by the ray's distance from the hole, and each prime is a derivative with respect to the angle the ray has swept around it. With the right-hand side set to zero the solution is a straight line. With it, light curves, loops, or falls in.

The image above solves this equation for every pixel of every frame, in about forty fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps per ray in the opening view. Each step applies the straight-line part exactly and approximates only the gravity term, and the steps shorten near the hole, where a ray is most sensitive to error. Switch off Bend light to delete the gravity term and see the same scene in flat space.

Aim a single ray

Light arrives from the left. Move over the diagram, or use the slider, to choose how far off-centre the ray is aimed. That distance is its impact parameter, b. Far out, light barely bends. At 2.598 rₛ it circles the hole forever. Any closer and it never comes back.

What is modelled, and what is not

In the picture

  • Exact light paths around a non-rotating black hole, including the thin photon ring of light that circled it.
  • The view of a camera hovering in place, with the stretching of its own rulers accounted for. That is why the shadow covers exactly half the sky at 1.5 rₛ.
  • Gas on circular orbits from 3 rₛ, the innermost stable orbit, out to 12 rₛ, coolest at the edges.
  • Colour from Planck's law and the CIE standard observer. Motion and gravity shift the light's frequency by a factor g, and a blackbody seen that way is a blackbody at g times the temperature.

Left out

  • Spin. Real black holes rotate, which flattens one side of the shadow and drags the disk inward.
  • Thickness. The disk is a flat sheet with painted-on turbulence, not a simulated flow.
  • Light travel time. You see the whole disk as it is now, not each part as it was when its light left.
  • Realistic heat. Gas this close to a real hole glows in ultraviolet and X-rays. The temperature here is chosen so the colours land in visible light.
  • The real sky. The stars and the band of the galaxy are generated, not catalogued.
